ASTRA-SHIELD — RESEARCH-INFORMED MODEL REVIEW
Reviewed: 2026-11-14. Scope: the original longitudinal synthetic-data models.
This review used existing code/results and primary research/official documentation.
No models were retrained, no final-test observations were opened, and no prediction
thresholds or dataset contents were changed for this review.

Assessment
The Random Forest is a defensible tabular-data baseline. The main weaknesses are
the target's scientific meaning, modest gain over persistence, incomplete temporal
validation, confidence/alert evaluation and explanations—not the lack of a neural
network. Benchmark research supports tree methods as strong tabular baselines;
it does not prove that any algorithm is best on this project [1].

What our existing evidence actually says
- Historical synthetic test Macro-F1: RF 0.5285 versus persistence 0.5181.
- Historical participant-bootstrap 95% interval for that gain: -0.0177 to +0.0370.
  This does not establish a reliable improvement over the simple baseline.
- Historical High-category precision 41.94%, recall 45.77%; many simulated High
  cases are missed and many predicted High cases are false alarms.
- More recent training-only grouped CV: RF mean Macro-F1 0.5163, persistence 0.5140.
  Eight fixed candidates were compared; none displaced the RF under that objective.
- Journal classification: historical synthetic test Macro-F1 0.3938; High recall
  0.1695. It should remain an experimental generated-language component.
- There are 120 virtual people, 84 in training. The 7,200 repeated daily records
  are not 7,200 independent people. All performance above describes the simulator.

Keep these parts
Explicit next-day labels; fixed participant-disjoint splits; train-fitted
preprocessing; past-only calibration/history; majority and persistence baselines;
per-class metrics; participant bootstrap; versioned experiments; genuine TreeSHAP
for the fatigue classifier; and clear synthetic provenance. Today's fatigue is a
legitimate input to tomorrow's outcome when it is available at the forecast cutoff.

Priority 1: define the outcome precisely
The primary outcome is next-day classification of a CUSTOM SIMULATED FATIGUE
SELF-REPORT. The low/moderate/high cutoffs at 4 and 7 are simulator choices. The
seven-day baseline is an engineering protocol, not a NASA-validated healthy range.
Research distinguishes subjective sleepiness/fatigue from objective performance;
in a controlled sleep-restriction experiment, cognitive deficits accumulated while
subjective sleepiness changed much less [2]. A forecast of our score does not
validate cognitive readiness, medical risk or mission safety.

Use research to justify domains such as sleep and schedule regularity [3], while
separating that rationale from empirical validation. Real-data validation needs
matched measurements, outcomes and a 24-hour horizon. Generic HRV datasets cannot
substitute for labeled longitudinal fatigue outcomes. More simulations—even with
a new random seed—cannot establish effectiveness on humans.

Priority 2: test a small, causal history-feature experiment
Keep the frozen 26-feature model as a control. Prespecify a compact extension:
  prior_3day_mean_fatigue = mean of fatigue at t-3, t-2, t-1;
  fatigue_change_vs_prior3 = today's fatigue minus that earlier mean;
  prior_3day_sleep_shortfall = sum(max(0, fixed_calibration_sleep - sleep_i))
                              for i in t-3, t-2, t-1;
  prior_7day_sleep_timing_variability = SD of earlier schedule-deviation values.
These are hypotheses, not improvements already demonstrated. The shortfall
feature is a baseline-relative proxy, not proven physiological sleep debt. Most
inputs can come from the canonical history now stored by the backend, without
new frontend measurements. Adequate history is required; do not fill missing days
with invented observations. Longer-term work could collect time since waking and
planned duty known at the cutoff. Actual future sleep/workload would be leakage.
Cumulative sleep-loss research motivates evaluating history rather than assuming
one night's duration is sufficient [2].

Priority 3: separate new-person validation from future-period validation
Our participant-disjoint evaluation answers whether a model transfers to different
virtual people after calibration. It does not test later mission conditions or
schedule drift. Keep that split and add a separately defined rolling-origin
assessment on development data. At each fitting cutoff, only use training outcomes
that were already available. Fit preprocessing, feature selection, calibration and
any threshold tuning inside the appropriate training folds [4].
Past measurements that would actually be available may be used to compute history;
blindly deleting all overlap between rolling histories is not the right rule.
Avoid concatenating all people and using a naive row-level TimeSeriesSplit. For a
claim about both unseen people and later dates, the protocol must enforce both.
Use nested or otherwise separated selection/evaluation if repeatedly tuning many
choices. The used test set remains historical; it is not a fresh test of revisions.

Priority 4: choose an alert objective, then evaluate probabilities
Macro-F1 reasonably weights the three categories, but does not alone define useful
alerting. In existing CV, the current RF has High recall 0.3756; balanced logistic
has High recall 0.6583 but lower Macro-F1 (0.4403). No universal winner follows.
Agree on the acceptable missed-case/false-alarm tradeoff, report High precision,
recall, PR curves and false alerts per person-week, and select an alert operating
point using development folds. A separate High-versus-rest alert threshold is an
option; sklearn's binary threshold utility is not automatically a three-class
decision rule [5]. Do not choose thresholds on the consumed final test.

Assess probability reliability using classwise reliability plots plus multiclass
Brier score/log loss. Compare a simple calibration method using held-out training
participants or grouped out-of-fold predictions, and evaluate the calibrated
system separately [6]. Calibration does not guarantee better Macro-F1 or real-world
validity. A 0–100 weighted class-severity index remains different from P(High).
If calibration wraps the classifier, update explanation scope: current TreeSHAP
explains the base classifier output, not automatically the calibrated output.

Priority 5: improve feature-importance interpretation
The prior top-five ranking is exploratory. The current calculation independently
permutes columns even though several satisfy identities such as current-baseline
= delta. Shuffling one column can create inconsistent feature combinations.
Correlated variables may also share predictive information and look unimportant
individually [7]. Prefer coherent feature-family comparisons or grouped-CV
ablations; preserve derived identities and inspect feasibility if permuting.
Three permutation repetitions do not quantify uncertainty across people. The
17-feature experiment's lower validation score is evidence from that comparison,
not proof that all 26 individual features are necessary.

TreeSHAP identifies contributions to this classifier's output; it does not identify
medical causes or prove that changing a feature will change an outcome [8]. Our
top-level three reasons explain ONLY the fatigue classifier. They do not explain
the Isolation Forest, either regressor, the journal classifier or overall health.

Priority 6: preserve measurement and component boundaries
The PVT-like simulator is a five-minute/60-response demonstration, not a separately
validated PVT implementation. Original studies show that PVT metrics and task
duration affect sensitivity; a validated brief task is not interchangeable with
an arbitrary duration or transformed score [9,10]. Do not convert the old
PVT_speed_seconds to median milliseconds without its actual definition. For future
real collection, preserve trial-level responses so valid lapse/response-speed
metrics can be calculated. HRV findings also depend on recording context/window;
one study's task-specific model cannot establish a universal RMSSD-fatigue rule [11].

Isolation Forest currently detects simulator-injected physiological outliers. The
fixed contamination value matches a simulator design choice, not an established
astronaut anomaly rate. Evaluate false alerts under exercise, recording artifacts,
baseline drift and missingness before making stronger claims. The generated journal
classifier needs separately annotated, representative text before real-language
claims. These components are displayed alongside fatigue forecasts; there is no
trained end-to-end fusion of all their outputs.

Recommended next implementation sequence
1. Freeze the intended outcome, deployment population, alert objective and current
   model as a control. Preserve existing source/metric chronology.
2. Compare the four history features above in bounded participant-group development
   experiments, alongside persistence and the existing RF. Add temporal evaluation.
3. Evaluate calibration and alert tradeoffs with separate training-fold data. A
   prespecified continuous/ordinal alternative is reasonable because labels are
   ordered, but thresholding a conditional mean may reduce rare-High recall.
4. Run missing-sensor, stale-data and shifted-baseline stress tests; explicitly
   abstain when required information is missing. Do not claim current strict-input
   rejection already demonstrates robust missing-data prediction.
5. Freeze a candidate and evaluate on a genuinely untouched, appropriately labeled
   cohort. Apply reporting guidance transparently; TRIPOD+AI is reporting guidance,
   not a certification or a substitute for methodological evaluation [12].

Avoid metric gaming: changing category cutoffs or removing valid difficult cases
just to raise F1; selecting a favorable random split; oversampling before splitting;
calling 0.5273 accuracy; treating additional synthetic rows as new human evidence;
claiming a more complex neural model must be better; or applying the old model to
new units/protocols by renaming columns. None addresses the main evidence gap.
